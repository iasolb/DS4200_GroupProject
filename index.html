Overview

Why 311 Data?

Municipal 311 systems provide a comprehensive, publicly available record of non-emergency issues reported by residents—from potholes and streetlight outages to code violations and trash complaints. Because these requests are georeferenced and timestamped, they offer a high-resolution lens into neighborhood conditions and priorities. Research shows that the structure of 311 requests serves as a meaningful signature of local socioeconomic context.

Wang et al. (2017) demonstrated that clustering neighborhoods by their 311 request patterns produces groups with distinctly different income, education, and demographic profiles. By comparing these "signatures" across a decade, we identify neighborhoods experiencing significant transformation through gentrification, infrastructure investment, or emerging distress patterns.

The 2015-2025 period represents a transformative decade for Boston, characterized by substantial Seaport District development, accelerated gentrification in historically lower-income neighborhoods like Roxbury and Dorchester, and increased climate-related infrastructure challenges. This project quantifies which neighborhoods changed most dramatically and interprets what those changes mean for Boston's urban landscape.

Dataset

About the Data

Our analysis uses Boston's publicly available 311 service request records from the City of Boston Open Data portal containing approximately 226,000 closed cases across 2 complete calendar years (2015 and 2025). Each record includes case metadata, geographic coordinates, timestamps, and neighborhood classifications.

Data Processing

We filtered both datasets to closed cases only and excluded categories less relevant to neighborhood condition assessment: parking enforcement, routine recycling, street cleaning, graffiti removal, animal control, abandoned vehicle enforcement, and traffic signal maintenance. The 11% reduction in filtered requests despite population growth may reflect improved municipal responsiveness, evolving civic engagement patterns, or migration to alternative reporting platforms.

Supplementary Data

We integrated American Community Survey (ACS) 5-Year estimates (2019-2023) providing census tract-level socioeconomic indicators (population, median household income, poverty rates). Geographic assignment via spatial join enables validation of 311 patterns against demographic context, following Wang et al.'s methodology.

Visualizations

Seven interactive and static visualizations explore neighborhood change from multiple perspectives

1 · Neighborhood Signature Drift Quantification

Goal: Identify which Boston neighborhoods changed most dramatically over the decade
Takeaway: East Boston, South Boston/Waterfront, and South Boston show the highest drift values—consistent with rapid Seaport development and gentrification. The median line helps identify neighborhoods experiencing above-average transformation.
Design Choices: Neighborhoods rank-ordered by drift magnitude for immediate pattern recognition. Color gradient (red = high change, green = low change) provides intuitive visual encoding. Cosine distance on y-axis quantifies compositional change independent of request volume.

2 · Interactive Side-by-Side Geographic Map

Goal: Compare spatial distribution of 311 requests across the decade
How to use: Click markers to view case details. Zoom and pan to explore specific areas. For advanced filtering, run python map_app.py locally.

Takeaway: Request density patterns reveal geographic clustering in high-traffic neighborhoods. Marker clustering enables exploration of spatial distributions while maintaining performance with thousands of data points.

3 · Neighborhood Request Composition Analysis

Goal: Show how different neighborhoods prioritize different types of service requests
How to use: Hover over bars to see exact request counts. Blue = 2015, Orange = 2025. Compare patterns across the six highest-volume neighborhoods.
Takeaway: Neighborhoods show distinct request profiles. Some prioritize trash/recycling complaints while others focus on infrastructure issues. Year-over-year shifts reveal changing neighborhood priorities and conditions.
Design Choices: Horizontal bars make long request-type names readable. Grouped bars (not stacked) enable direct count comparison. Color legend clearly distinguishes years. Top 5 request types per neighborhood focus on dominant patterns.

4 · Interactive Neighborhood Request Pattern Comparison

Goal: Enable detailed exploration of individual neighborhood request patterns · Altair
How to use: Select any neighborhood from the dropdown. Click the legend (2015/2025) to toggle years on/off. Hover for exact counts. Request types appear alphabetically.
Takeaway: Enables granular examination of how specific neighborhoods' complaint profiles evolved. Alphabetical sorting makes finding specific request types quick. Toggle functionality allows focusing on single-year patterns.
Design Choices: Dropdown selection reduces visual clutter while maintaining access to all neighborhoods. Grouped bars with vertical offset prevent overlap. Legend-based filtering provides intuitive year comparison control.

5 · Seasonal Patterns in 311 Requests

Goal: Reveal temporal patterns and seasonal variations in request types
Takeaway: Snow removal requests spike dramatically in winter months (expected), while infrastructure issues like street lighting and potholes remain relatively consistent year-round. Temporal consistency across the decade suggests core municipal service demands persist despite neighborhood transformations.
Design Choices: Heatmap format efficiently displays 3 dimensions (request type × month × count). Sequential colormap (YlOrRd) intuitively encodes intensity. Side-by-side comparison highlights decade-long changes in seasonal patterns.

6 · Cluster Distribution Comparison

Goal: Show how neighborhoods group by similar 311 request patterns using k-means clustering
How to use: Hover over bars to see exact neighborhood counts per cluster. Compare how cluster sizes changed between 2015 and 2025.
Takeaway: Neighborhood redistribution across clusters indicates systematic reorganization of Boston's service request landscape. Some clusters grew while others shrank, reflecting citywide demographic and economic transitions.
Design Choices: K-means with k=4 follows Wang et al.'s Boston methodology. Side-by-side bars enable direct cluster size comparison. Consistent cluster colors maintain identity across years.

7 · Tract-Level Socioeconomic Validation

Goal: Validate neighborhood patterns with tract-level census data · D3.js
How to use: Use the dropdown to switch y-axis metrics. Hover for tract details. Click a point to pin the highlight. Point size = population, color = median income.
Takeaway: Poverty rate shows weak positive correlation (r = 0.13) with complaint change. Modest tract-level associations suggest 311 captures some socioeconomic structure but shouldn't be interpreted as definitive causal evidence.
Design Choices: Switchable y-axis metrics enable exploration of multiple relationships in one chart. Point size and color encode additional dimensions (population, income). Click-to-pin functionality supports detailed inspection.

Conclusion

What We Learned

Boston's 311 request patterns reveal that neighborhood change is not evenly distributed. The strongest signal comes from changes in complaint composition and neighborhood structure rather than simple volume trends or single tract-level correlations.

Uneven Transformation

East Boston, South Boston/Waterfront, and South Boston show especially large signature shifts—consistent with rapid Seaport development and gentrification pressures documented in these areas.

Declining Volume

11% reduction in requests despite population growth may indicate improved municipal responsiveness, shifting engagement patterns, or migration to alternative reporting channels.

Seasonal Stability

Core service demands (snow removal, potholes, street lighting) show remarkable temporal consistency, suggesting fundamental municipal needs persist despite neighborhood transformations.

Cluster Reorganization

Neighborhood redistribution across clusters indicates systematic citywide reorganization with implications for resource allocation and service delivery planning.

Future Work

Future analysis could incorporate additional years between 2015-2025, response-time metrics, and cross-referencing with housing, zoning, or transit development data to distinguish whether shifts reflect demographic turnover, infrastructure investment, or changing reporting behavior.
